The first 90 days set the tone for everything after. Most of the damage in a new branch is done in the first year, and a lot of it in the first few weeks. Use this plan to stay on top of the basics, and resist the urge to change everything at once.

## Before day one

- [ ] Insurance in place from the moment you complete
- [ ] Payroll ready to run under your name
- [ ] Bank account and card terminal set up
- [ ] Handover date and time agreed with the seller, and who will be there
- [ ] Post Office's week of counter training done. It's compulsory, but it happens in a classroom in London on a practice system with no real customers, so plan time on a live counter with an experienced postmaster too
- [ ] Staff list, contracts and holiday balances in hand
- [ ] P250 pack sent for every member of staff who will serve on the counter, with a basic disclosure and right to work check for each. No approval means no Horizon login, and no login means they can't use the system
- [ ] Your reserve untouched and ready

## Days 1 to 7: cash, compliance and calm

This week is about control. Know what you've got, lock it down, and let the team see you're steady.

**Cash and compliance**

- [ ] Handover day: expect the branch to be closed for most of the day while Post Office auditors count every bit of cash, every stamp and all the stock
- [ ] Have your own independent check of that count before you sign. Auditors are human and they do make mistakes. Once you've signed, a shortfall found that evening or the next day is yours
- [ ] Any losses or gains from the audit are settled by the outgoing postmaster before the branch is signed over
- [ ] Sign for the safe keys, alarm codes and access codes, and check each one works
- [ ] Change locks, alarm codes and safe codes
- [ ] Check who has keys and take back any you don't need out there
- [ ] Balance every day, without exception
- [ ] Learn your cash delivery and collection days, usually once or twice a week, and hold only the cash you need between them
- [ ] Log any discrepancy the same day, however small
- [ ] Check the security kit works: alarm, CCTV, shutters, panic buttons

**Staff**

- [ ] Meet the whole team on day one
- [ ] Tell them what's happening, and what isn't. Under TUPE their terms carry on, so say so
- [ ] Change nothing yet. Listen, watch and take notes
- [ ] Check the rota covers every opening hour

**Customers**

- [ ] Take control of the Google Business Profile, or claim it if nobody has
- [ ] Check opening times, photos, and products and services listed are right
- [ ] Be on the shop floor. Regulars want to see who the new owner is

**Suppliers**

- [ ] Take meter readings for gas, electricity and water on completion day
- [ ] Put utilities, broadband, phone and security contracts into your name
- [ ] List every supplier with contract end dates and notice periods

## Weeks 2 to 4: learn the branch

Now you know what you've got. This is where you find out how it really works.

**Cash and compliance**

- [ ] Review every transaction correction so far. Look for patterns by person, product or time
- [ ] Check that everyone on the counter has had the training they need
- [ ] Walk through your cash and stock procedures with the team, and write them down

**Staff**

- [ ] One-to-one with every member of staff. What do they think works, what doesn't, and what would they change?
- [ ] Note who knows what. Where are you relying on one person?
- [ ] Check holiday already booked, and plan cover

**Customers and reviews**

- [ ] Read every review of the branch, especially recent ones. That's your free list of what to fix first
- [ ] Reply to recent reviews, good and bad, politely and quickly
- [ ] Put a QR code at the counter so your regulars can leave a review easily
- [ ] Introduce yourself to the businesses either side

**Suppliers**

- [ ] Get quotes on anything close to renewal. Don't let deals roll over on worse terms
- [ ] Sort out old, damaged or out-of-date stock
- [ ] Check card fees and bank charges against your volumes

**The numbers**

- [ ] Set up a simple weekly view: sessions, retail takings, wages, and cash position
- [ ] Compare your first weeks with what the seller's figures suggested

## Months 2 and 3: settle and improve

By now you should know the branch. This is where you start making it yours, carefully.

**Cash and compliance**

- [ ] Still balancing every day. This never stops
- [ ] Spot check your own processes. Are they being followed when you're not there?

**Staff**

- [ ] Look at the staffing structure against the hours the branch is busy
- [ ] If anything needs to change, take advice before you act. TUPE doesn't disappear after a few weeks. Speak to an HR adviser before you change any transferred member of staff's terms
- [ ] For new hires, build clear customer service expectations into their contracts
- [ ] Set simple goals for each person and review them monthly

**Customers**

- [ ] Fix the top two or three things customers complain about
- [ ] Keep asking regulars for reviews. The happy ones rarely leave one unless you ask
- [ ] Look at what customers ask for that you don't offer

**Suppliers**

- [ ] Review every contract you've inherited. Keep what works and replace what doesn't

**The numbers**

- [ ] Go through your first two or three remuneration statements line by line. Which products are growing and which are slipping?
- [ ] Compare actual costs with the hidden costs worksheet you filled in before you bought
- [ ] Check your reserve. How much have you used, and on what?
- [ ] Write down what you'd do differently in the next quarter

## Take one day out

Once things are steady, take one day a week out of the branch. Not for golf and not for admin. Use it to watch, think and plan. When you're in the deep end, a week goes, then a month, then a year, and you've been busy but haven't moved forward. Step back and look at the branch the way a customer does.

## The things that go wrong most

- Changing staff terms too quickly
- Not balancing every day
- Ignoring online reviews
- Letting the reserve drift into everyday spending
- Trying to fix everything in month one
