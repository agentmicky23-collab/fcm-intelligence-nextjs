When you buy a Post Office with staff, the staff come with it. That's TUPE, and it catches out more first-time buyers than almost anything else. This is the plain version of what you need to know. It isn't legal advice, so get a solicitor to check your deal.

## What TUPE is

TUPE is short for the Transfer of Undertakings (Protection of Employment) Regulations. It applies when a business, or part of one, moves from one owner to another as a going concern. Buying a Post Office and its shop from the current operator will usually count. [CHECK: with a solicitor for each deal]

The idea is simple. Staff shouldn't lose out just because the business changed hands. So their jobs move to you on the same terms.

**One important exception.** If you buy the shares of the company that owns the branch, the employer doesn't change. The company is still the employer, so TUPE doesn't apply in the same way. But everything the company owes, including to staff, stays with the company, which you now own. Either way, you inherit the history. [CHECK: share purchase vs asset purchase with a solicitor]

## What transfers to you

- **The staff** who work in the business being sold.
- **Their contracts**, with the same pay, hours, holiday and other terms.
- **Their length of service.** Someone who has worked there for 12 years has 12 years' service with you on day one.
- **Holiday they've built up** and not taken.
- **Most liabilities connected to them**, such as unpaid wages or claims that relate to their employment before the sale.

Pensions are more complicated. Some pension rights don't transfer in full, but you'll have your own duties as an employer, including auto-enrolment. [CHECK: pension position for each deal with an accountant or pensions adviser]

## What you can't change

- **You can't dismiss someone because of the transfer.** A dismissal where the main reason is the transfer is likely to be automatically unfair.
- **You can't change their terms just because of the transfer.** If the main reason for a change is the transfer, it's likely to be void, even if the employee agrees to it.

There are limited exceptions where there's an "economic, technical or organisational reason entailing changes in the workforce". That's a legal test, not a general excuse. Don't rely on it without advice. [CHECK: current ETO guidance]

## What you can change

- **Things that have nothing to do with the transfer.** Normal business changes made for genuine business reasons can still happen, following a fair process, once you're running the branch.
- **Changes the contract already allows.** Some contracts let the employer change rotas or duties. Read them.
- **Restructures with a proper reason and a fair process.** If the branch genuinely needs fewer hours, that can be dealt with. Expect to pay redundancy based on each person's full service. [CHECK: statutory redundancy qualifying period and limits]
- **New staff you hire** are on whatever contract you set. This is where you build in accountability, such as customer service expectations your team is measured against.

My rule: change nothing on day one. Get to know the team first.

## Information you're entitled to

The seller has to give you "employee liability information" before the transfer. [CHECK: the current deadline, which I understand to be at least 28 days before completion] It should include:

- Who is transferring, and their ages
- Their written terms of employment
- Any disciplinary action or grievances in the last two years [CHECK]
- Any claims or legal action, and any they reasonably expect
- Any collective agreements

Don't wait for the legal minimum. Ask for a full staff list with pay, hours, start dates and holiday balances at the start of due diligence.

## Informing and consulting staff

Both you and the seller have duties to inform, and sometimes consult, the staff affected before the transfer. You have to tell the seller about any "measures" you plan to take that affect them, so the seller can pass that on. In a small business the staff can sometimes be informed directly rather than through representatives. [CHECK: current rules for small businesses]

Get this wrong and there can be compensation to pay per employee, and both buyer and seller can be on the hook. [CHECK: current maximum award]

## The costs that come with TUPE

| Cost | What to check |
|---|---|
| Untaken holiday | Balances for each person, in writing. Negotiate into the price. |
| Redundancy exposure | Length of service for each person. Long service means a bigger bill if you ever restructure. |
| Pay and pensions | Current pay and pension contributions, and the next legal minimum wage rise. |
| Outstanding claims | Any disputes or claims the seller knows about. |
| Legal and payroll setup | Getting contracts reviewed and payroll moved over. |

Ask your solicitor about protections in the sale agreement, such as warranties and indemnities from the seller, or holding back part of the price. [CHECK: with solicitor]

## Practical steps

- [ ] Ask for the staff list with pay, hours, start dates and holiday at the first serious conversation
- [ ] Get copies of every contract, and check who actually works in the branch
- [ ] Ask about disciplinary issues, grievances and claims
- [ ] Work out the redundancy exposure, even if you don't plan to use it
- [ ] Agree holiday balances in writing and reflect them in the price
- [ ] Tell the seller in writing what, if anything, you plan to change
- [ ] Get your solicitor to review the sale agreement for staff protections
- [ ] Plan payroll from completion, and check right to work records [CHECK: time allowed for a new employer to carry out its own checks after a TUPE transfer]
- [ ] Complete a P250 pack for every member of staff who will use the Post Office system. Post Office won't handle HR or vet your staff for you, so the P250 needs each person's full details, a basic disclosure (criminal record check) and proof of their right to work in the UK. Until Post Office approves it, they get no Horizon login, and without a login they can't serve on the counter
- [ ] Meet the team on day one. Reassure them, listen, and change nothing yet

## A note on changing law

Employment law in the UK is changing, including some qualifying periods and rights. Check the current position with a solicitor or HR adviser before you rely on any threshold. [CHECK: status of recent employment law reforms at time of publishing]
